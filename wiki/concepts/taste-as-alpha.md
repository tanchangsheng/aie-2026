# Taste as Alpha

## Definition

The claim that taste — the human capacity to make high-quality qualitative judgments before a metric exists — is the primary decay-resistant career differentiator in an era of capable AI agents.

Two terms structure the argument:

- **Alpha**: what makes you meaningfully better than what current models can do
- **Decay**: how fast the models catch up

Most engineering capabilities are decaying fast. Taste decays slowly, and resets with every model release.

## What Taste Is

Mitchell Hashimoto's definition (Jun 26, 2026, 706K views):

"Taste is the ability to consistently make high-quality qualitative judgments where no objective metric exists. It's the creation of something that feels right intuitively, with no real justifiable way to measure that. But when you do it, people feel it. A person with good taste is someone who can do this repeatedly, consistently. The funny thing about taste is that it's hard to create, but its result is very easy to copy. Once someone makes a tasteful decision, others can imitate it almost immediately."

Paul Graham (Feb 14, 2026, 2.1M views): "In the AI age, taste will become even more important. When anyone can make anything, the big differentiator is what you choose to make." (paulgraham.com/taste.html)

Osmani's synthesis: Taste is the judgment before the metric exists. Hard to create. Easy to copy. Valuable because it decides what everyone copies next.

## The Decay Curve

Not all capabilities decay at the same rate:

| Capability | Status |
|---|---|
| **Speed** | Gone — agents write 600 lines while you find coffee |
| **Recall** | Gone — it's a context window now |
| **Verification** | Automating — "we were the weak link" |
| **Taste** | Alpha — resets every release, takes longer to decay |
| **Judgment** | A slope rather than a wall |

The headline: "The half life of an edge is a release. The half life of a signature is a career."

Accountability, framed as a capability, stays flat — it doesn't decay at all because it's not a model capability to begin with. It's a structural property of who owns consequences.

## Why Taste Resets Every Release

Unlike speed or recall, taste isn't a performance metric that models can be benchmarked on. A model can be prompted to explain what makes something tasteful, but the model cannot consistently be the first to exercise taste on genuinely new problems — because taste by definition operates before the training signal exists. Each model release may close the gap, but the frontier resets. The people with taste define what the next model is trained to approximate.

## The "Anyone Can Make Anything" Inflection

This is the conference's strongest statement about what changes when code generation becomes trivially cheap. When generation is cheap, curation and selection become the bottleneck. In software: choosing what to build, which problem to solve, which UX is elegant — these are taste decisions, and they determine what the engineering effort amplifies.

## Tension with Other Views

The Tížková/Factory framing from Day 2 says "humans decide what to build, agents handle how." Osmani's taste argument is the most detailed elaboration of what "deciding what to build" actually requires at the individual level. The two framings are complementary: the factory is an organizational pattern; taste is a cognitive skill the factory's human operators need.

An open tension: Hashimoto notes that taste is "hard to create, but its result is very easy to copy." If AI models are trained on the outputs of tasteful decisions, does the gap between human taste and model taste shrink faster than Osmani's "resets every release" framing implies?

## Open Questions

- Is taste trainable, or is it an aptitude? If trainable, what practices develop it — and does heavy AI use atrophy it (see [Cognitive Debt](cognitive-debt.md))?
- The decay curve shows taste decaying slowly but still decaying. At what point does model taste for well-defined aesthetic domains (e.g., UI layout, API design) become good enough that human taste provides marginal alpha?
- The "easy to copy" property of taste results means first-mover advantage is short. Does that shorten the career half-life of taste as a differentiator?
